This page is a reading guide for anyone who wants the sources in plain language. That includes people waiting on an assessment, people who were given a different diagnosis first, families, students, and clinicians. The door is open. A diagnosis for any one person belongs with a clinician who can see the whole history. So does any decision to start, stop, or change medication or another treatment.

## What the spectrum is

In October 2026 the UK government published an [independent review of mental health conditions, ADHD, and autism](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/independent-review-into-mental-health-conditions-adhd-and-autism-final-report/independent-review-into-mental-health-conditions-adhd-and-autism-executive-summary). The review is about England. It describes autism and ADHD as neurodevelopmental conditions, strongly influenced by genetics and present from early life. They shape attention, communication, behaviour, learning, and the way a person experiences the world. With the right adjustments many people live full lives. Some face substantial difficulties and lifelong high support needs.

The [National Autistic Society, in written evidence to Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/136767/pdf/), states the category in fewer words: autism is not a mental health disorder. Autistic people are more likely than other people to develop a mental health problem. The Society's evidence says around 70% will have a mental health disorder during their lifetimes, and that wellbeing is also shaped by whether the rest of life is set up so an autistic person can take part.

The review's account of the spectrum is specific. Traits connected with these conditions, including inattention, social difficulty, and differences in emotional regulation, occur in degrees across the whole population. Most people are sometimes inattentive, anxious, or socially uncomfortable. Recognizing those traits, or describing yourself as neurodivergent, names a kind of human variation. A clinical diagnosis asks a further question: whether the difficulties started in early development, persist, show up across settings, and impair everyday functioning enough to meet criteria.

DSM-5 and ICD-11 are part of why the public picture changed. They combined older autism subtypes into a single spectrum diagnosis, and they formally recognized that autism and ADHD can be diagnosed in the same person. Some people who would once have received a different label, or no label, now meet criteria. The review treats that broadening as one reason recorded diagnoses rose, and as an incomplete explanation of how large the rise has been.

There is no biological test that returns a yes or no. Diagnosis stays a clinical judgement, drawing together history, more than one setting, and the degree of impairment. The review keeps four words apart, because services have been folding them into each other: distress, disorder, diagnosis, and need. Real distress can exist without meeting criteria for a disorder. A diagnosis can be personally meaningful and still fail to describe the support a person needs in school, work, or daily life. In the review's wording, neurodiversity is a social term for the ordinary breadth of human variation. It is a welcome public language. It is not itself a diagnosis, and a product sold as a "neurodiversity assessment" blurs that line.

The numbers in the review are cautious, and they are worth reading that way. The best England estimate it cites for autism among young people remains about 1.2%, from the 2017 Mental Health of Children and Young People survey. There is no equivalent recent adult population survey. Recorded diagnoses tell a different story. Administrative prevalence among children aged 6 to 11 rose from 0.24% in 2000 to 4.58% in 2025, more than twenty-fold, and the rise among ages 12 to 17 was steeper still. By December 2025 more than 254,000 people had an open autism referral, with over 90% waiting longer than 13 weeks. The review says that surge does not, by itself, prove overdiagnosis at scale. The population estimates are old, the methods differ, and they are not a fixed biological ceiling. People presenting now are not less impaired than people identified before. What the review does say is that the balance of risk is shifting. Under-recognition, including of girls and women, was the long-standing failure. As demand outruns careful assessment, misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis become more important. All three can exist in the same system.

## Three ways a label goes wrong

The review separates three failures. It calls each of them a system failure, and it says they are not unique to autism.

**Underdiagnosis.** A person has the condition and does not receive the diagnosis that would open the right support. Historical under-recognition of girls and women is the clearest example in the review: at least part of the old sex difference was a difference in who was seen, rather than a difference in how common the condition is. Under-recognition of some groups continues.

**Misdiagnosis.** The diagnosis is the wrong one, or only part of the picture. A missing second diagnosis counts. Mental health conditions, ADHD, autism, trauma, sleep problems, and adversity can produce overlapping symptoms, and people very often live with several of them at once. The review's examples run in both directions. Someone may be diagnosed with autism while clinically significant anxiety is overlooked. Undiagnosed ADHD may be mistaken for anxiety or depression alone.

**Overdiagnosis.** A label for a condition the person does not have, or for a difficulty that would not have caused harm if it had stayed undiscovered. The review also includes turning ordinary life strain into a medical problem by widening definitions or lowering thresholds without evidence that health improves. It says plainly that current evidence cannot show overdiagnosis happening at scale, because population data and reporting are too thin. It also says the rapid rise in assessments, together with system failures, is creating the conditions for more misdiagnoses.

The risk of misdiagnosis is higher when an assessment is brief, not face to face, fragmented, made without informants or records, or focused on a single condition. The risk of misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis also rises when a person's difficulties overlap, assessment quality varies, information is withheld, thresholds are applied inconsistently, or a diagnosis is the main door to help in education, work, or welfare.

Co-occurrence is the ordinary presentation, which is why a single-condition pathway keeps missing people. The review reports that by 2024, among 16 to 24 year olds with a common mental disorder, 57% also screened positive for ADHD and 61% for autistic traits. A positive screen means a raised likelihood. It is not a confirmed diagnosis.

## When autism is the diagnosis that arrives late

A second set of sources looks at autism recognized in adolescence or adulthood, after a mental health diagnosis the person later understands as wrong or incomplete.

[Kentrou and colleagues, in *eClinicalMedicine* in 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(24)00165-2/fulltext), asked autistic adults about earlier psychiatric diagnoses. The archived text is also on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11001629/). About a quarter of participants, 24.6% (298 people), reported at least one previous psychiatric diagnosis they regarded as a misdiagnosis. Personality disorders were the most frequent, followed by anxiety disorders, mood disorders, chronic fatigue or burnout-related diagnoses, and ADHD. Women in the study were more susceptible than men to a personality-disorder diagnosis they did not accept. The paper cites estimates that 70 to 80% of autistic adults meet criteria for at least one other psychiatric condition, and earlier research in which only about half of autistic adults agreed with their co-occurring diagnoses. It also discusses a study of 1,507 autistic adults that found higher rates of anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia than in sex-matched non-autistic adults, and a five-fold rate of suicide attempts. Those figures are why missed and partial diagnoses matter. They describe groups in studies, and they are a reason to get careful care. If you are in immediate danger, contact local emergency services. In the UK and Ireland you can call Samaritans at 116 123. In the United States you can call or text 988.

The paper is careful with the word misdiagnosis. Some people experienced a mental health diagnosis as wrong because they understood the symptoms as autistic characteristics, or as the result of living without the right support, even where those symptoms might still meet formal criteria for a second condition. A perceived misdiagnosis and a real second condition both belong in the picture. An [Oxford lay summary of related work](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:b4dbf634-3f96-4dc7-b06f-d28d76397925/files/m565afd4872d0b2b2836fce71420b04ca) puts the disagreement in those two reasons: autistic characteristics were confused with a mental health condition, and the mental health difficulty was understood as coming from the strain of being autistic. Participants traced that to clinicians with too little autism knowledge, and to diagnoses that were not talked through. The summary recommends autism training, tools and treatment adapted for autistic people, and clearer communication.

Two case reports show the same confusion in a single life. They are not rates. [One report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9036173/) describes a young autistic woman who came through emergency care, was treated as psychotic, and was admitted involuntarily, until a fuller history during the admission identified autism. The authors discuss camouflaging, the masking of social difficulty so that current criteria and adult tools such as ADOS-2 Module 4 can miss it, and the way depression and anxiety can look unusual in autistic adults. More than half of autistic people in the literature they cite have a psychiatric condition alongside autism, and a crisis is often what brings someone to a clinic. [A second report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8855062/) describes someone first diagnosed with borderline personality disorder. Clinicians experienced with autism later diagnosed autism, ADD (the report's term), and depression, and did not diagnose a personality disorder. The mechanisms it names are a narrow assessment, too little autism knowledge in general mental health services, and co-occurring self-injury, depression, and attentional difficulties read as the entire explanation.

A [National Elf Service summary](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/learning-disabilities/autistic-spectrum-disorder/autistic-adults-bpd-misdiagnosis/) of qualitative research with autistic adults who had carried a borderline personality disorder diagnosis describes the pattern in participants' own accounts. Many felt autism had been missed because of gender bias and thin training, including among women and gender minorities who have historically been under-diagnosed. Self-injury, autistic shutdowns, and the effects of childhood trauma were filed under a personality disorder. A later autism diagnosis often brought relief, self-acceptance, and a better way to manage health. Adequate mental healthcare was still hard to reach: disbelief, delayed referrals, and long waits showed up again after the autism diagnosis.

Two *Psychology Today* essays, [one from 2026](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/a-healing-community/202606/5-misdiagnoses-of-autism-and-why-they-happen) and [one from 2023](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/women-with-autism-spectrum-disorder/202304/misdiagnosis-of-autism-for-mental-and-personality), are commentary on this literature rather than new studies. They are useful as a plain-language map of overlapping labels, including mood disorders, ADHD, and personality disorders, and for one practical line: a person who suspects a missed autism diagnosis can take that question to their own clinician. Changing or stopping treatment belongs in that conversation, with medical support at every step.

The National Autistic Society's evidence names the habit underneath these accounts: diagnostic overshadowing, in which an autistic trait is attributed to a mental health disorder, or a mental health problem is missed because everything is folded into autism. People told the Society that the main barrier inside mental health services is a lack of autism understanding. Some autistic adults, the Society says, have been treated for severe mental illnesses they were later found not to have.

## What a careful reading asks for

The sources agree on the shape of a better assessment, even where they cannot yet measure how large each error is.

A careful assessment is face to face. It uses developmental history, people who knew the person earlier, and records. It looks for more than one condition. It can tell a trait, a crisis, and a second diagnosis apart. General mental health services need enough autism knowledge to do that, because many autistic adults arrive there first, for anxiety, depression, or a crisis, rather than for an autism assessment.

The England review asks services to move from a diagnosis-dependent system to what it calls needs-led plus diagnosis. Help that matches a person's needs can start before, during, and after an assessment, so support is not locked behind a waiting list for a label. Diagnosis still matters when impairment is significant. An accurate one can explain a life and open the right care. An inaccurate or partial one can point at the wrong treatment and hide something else that is also true. The review wants success measured by participation in relationships, education, work, and community, alongside quality of life, rather than by assessments and waiting lists alone.

Both risks stay live. Girls, women, and other people who camouflage are still missed. A high-volume system that assesses quickly, at a distance, or for one condition at a time can also stamp the wrong label, or a label that is only half the story. The review's standard for either failure is the same: a comprehensive assessment, a timely diagnosis where the criteria are met, and practical support that does not wait on the paperwork.

## The door is open

This page will not tell you whether you, or someone you love, is autistic. It can hand you the documents. The executive summary, the Kentrou paper, and the National Autistic Society evidence are the place to start. Take what fits to a clinician who will look at more than one possibility. If you already work in a service, the same pages are a request for assessments that can hold overlap, and for autism knowledge in the rooms where people already show up.
